VAT Lesson 4 VAT Rates and Types of Supplies An analysis of the different rates of VAT applicable in Zimbabwe, covering the standard rate, zero-rated supplies, exempt supplies, and the policy reasoning behind differential rating, with reference to current legislative provisions.
1

Context

VAT does not apply at a single rate to all supplies. Zimbabwe's VAT system operates a multi-rate structure where the applicable rate depends on the nature of the supply, giving rise to both planning opportunities and compliance obligations.

2

Legislation

The standard rate is prescribed under Section 6(1) of the VAT Act. Schedule 1 identifies exempt supplies while Schedule 2 lists zero-rated supplies. Finance Act amendments periodically adjust which items fall in each category.

3

Concepts

This lesson covers the standard rate, zero-rated and exempt supplies, the policy rationale for differential rating, the practical impact on input tax recovery, and the VAT treatment of mixed supplies.

4.1 Standard-Rated Supplies 4.2 Zero-Rated Supplies 4.3 Exempt Supplies

4.1 Standard-Rated Supplies

Standard-rated supplies are those taxed at the general (standard) rate of VAT. In Zimbabwe, the standard VAT rate is 15% of the value of the supply. (Note: The standard rate was temporarily reduced to 14.5% in 2020 but reverted to 15% in 2023; it is further set to increase to 15.5% from 1 January 2026.) This general rate of VAT applies to the majority of sales of goods and services, unless a specific provision zero-rates the item or exempts it from VAT. In other words, most everyday commercial transactions – from selling manufactured products to providing ordinary services – are subject to 15% VAT by default.

When a supply is standard-rated, VAT is charged on the selling price and the supplier must add 15% VAT to the price paid by the consumer. The supplier (if a registered operator) then remits this output tax to the Zimbabwe Revenue Authority (ZIMRA), but can claim credit for any VAT paid on inputs used to make the supply (input tax credit). This mechanism ensures VAT is ultimately paid by the end-consumer, while businesses act as intermediaries who collect and pass on the tax to the government.

Example (Domestic Sale): A local electronics store sells a television for ZWL 1,000. Being a standard-rated good, a 15% VAT of ZWL 150 is added, so the customer pays ZWL 1,150 in total. The store must issue a tax invoice and will later remit the ZWL 150 VAT to ZIMRA, but it can offset any input VAT it paid on buying the TV from its supplier (thus recovering VAT on its costs). This illustrates how the standard rate is applied in practice to consumer sales.

Example (Importation): Standard-rated VAT also applies to imported goods. If a Zimbabwean company imports machinery valued at USD 10,000, it will be charged 15% VAT (USD 1,500) at the point of entry by customs. The importer pays this import VAT to ZIMRA before the goods are released. If the importing company is VAT-registered, it can later claim that USD 1,500 as input tax credit on its VAT return (since the machinery will be used in making taxable supplies). This ensures imported products bear the same 15% tax as locally supplied goods, maintaining a level playing field.

In summary, the standard rate of VAT is the default tax rate on goods and services in Zimbabwe. Practical examples of standard-rated supplies are abundant: a supermarket selling toiletries, a furniture shop selling chairs, a consulting firm billing a local client for services – all these transactions include 15% VAT in the price. Even certain items that were once zero-rated have been moved into the standard-rated category over time as tax policy evolved. For instance, products like butter, various nuts (almonds, peanuts), margarine, gloves, and raincoats were previously zero-rated but became standard-rated at 15% from 2010 onwards. This change means consumers now pay VAT on those items, reflecting how the government can adjust the scope of standard-rated goods.

4.2 Zero-Rated Supplies

Zero-rated supplies are taxable supplies that attract VAT at a rate of 0%. This 0% rate means no output VAT is charged to the customer, but importantly the supplier is still entitled to claim input tax credits on their business purchases related to that supply. In effect, zero-rating removes the VAT cost for the end-consumer and relieves the supplier of any VAT burden on inputs. The purpose of zero-rating is often to encourage the supply of certain essential or export goods by making them completely free of VAT throughout the supply chain.

Under the Zimbabwean VAT Act, zero-rated supplies are explicitly specified (typically in the VAT Act schedules or regulations). Key categories of zero-rated goods include basic necessities and exports. For example, the law zero-rates basic foodstuffs (staple foods) so that consumers are not burdened by extra cost on essentials. Basic commodities like staple maize meal (mealie-meal), sugar, bread, cooking oil, milk, meat, and salt are zero-rated in Zimbabwe. This means when you buy a loaf of bread or a bag of mealie-meal from a store, no VAT is charged on it, helping keep such essential food affordable. Similarly, certain agricultural inputs are zero-rated to support farming; fertilizers, seeds, pesticides, animal feed and remedy, and farm equipment like tractors or planters can be zero-rated supplies, allowing farmers to acquire these without paying VAT (which supports food production and food security).

Another major class of zero-rated supplies is exports. Exported goods (goods delivered outside Zimbabwe) are taxed at 0% to ensure they leave the country free of VAT. This is standard international practice – VAT is a tax on domestic consumption, so exports are untaxed to make local products competitive abroad. For instance, if a manufacturer in Zimbabwe sells machinery to a buyer in South Africa, that sale can be zero-rated as an export: the Zimbabwean company will charge 0% VAT on the invoice (no VAT to the foreign customer), yet it can still claim credits for any Zimbabwean VAT it paid on inputs (like raw materials) used to produce the machinery. The result is that the exporter can refund its input VAT and the foreign buyer faces no Zimbabwean VAT, thereby avoiding double taxation and encouraging exports. (One exception in Zimbabwe’s law is certain unprocessed minerals: for example, un-beneficiated chrome exports are specifically taxed at 15% instead of 0% to discourage export of raw minerals.)

Exported services are also generally zero-rated. This refers to services supplied by a Zimbabwean firm to a client outside Zimbabwe. For the service to qualify, it usually must be used or enjoyed outside Zimbabwe or the benefit is realized abroad. Examples of zero-rated exported services could include a local consulting company providing advice to an overseas client, an IT firm in Harare developing software for an overseas business, or an architect designing a project for a foreign customer. These services, when supplied to non-resident clients, can be zero-rated for VAT purposes. For instance, if a Zimbabwean accounting firm prepares a report for a company in the UK, it would charge 0% VAT on its fee because the service is for a foreign client (an exported service). This zero-rating of services ensures that Zimbabwean service providers can compete internationally – their foreign clients are not charged Zimbabwean VAT (which those clients couldn’t recover), and the local provider can still reclaim any local VAT on inputs (e.g. on office expenses incurred in producing the service).

It’s crucial to understand that zero-rated supplies are still “taxable supplies” in the eyes of the law, just taxed at 0%. Therefore, all the compliance obligations (issuing tax invoices, record-keeping, filing returns) remain, and critically the supplier must adhere to documentary proof requirements to justify applying the 0% rate. A registered operator who supplies zero-rated goods or services must obtain and retain documentary proof of the entitlement to zero-rate that supply. In practice, this means if you are zero-rating an export, you need to have evidence such as export documents, bills of lading or airway bills, customs clearance certificates, or proof of delivery outside the country. For zero-rated local supplies (like basic foods), you should ensure those items are indeed listed as zero-rated by law. Documentary proof is especially important for exports: without proper export documentation, ZIMRA may refuse the zero-rating and treat the sale as domestic (standard-rated) requiring the 15% VAT to be paid. The VAT General Regulations explicitly require production of such proof for exports to qualify as zero-rated, underlining that the onus is on the supplier to prove the goods or services left Zimbabwe or met the conditions for zero-rating.

Risks of misapplication: Suppliers must be very careful not to misapply zero-rating. Incorrectly zero-rating a sale that doesn’t qualify (or failing to keep the necessary proof) can lead to serious consequences. If ZIMRA audits the business and finds that a supposedly zero-rated supply should have been standard-rated, the supplier will be liable to pay the unpaid VAT (output tax that should have been charged) retroactively, often with interest and penalties. In fact, applying a zero rate or exempt status to a supply that is actually standard-rated is considered a compliance error that flags a tax audit – it’s seen as an “incorrect application of the rules”. For example, if a company zero-rates a local sale improperly or cannot produce export documents for a zero-rated export, ZIMRA can assess the 15% VAT on that transaction. The company would then have to pay that VAT out of pocket (since it didn’t collect it from the customer), plus potential penalties for underpayment. Thus, the risk of misapplication is financial loss and sanctions; businesses should always double-check the VAT treatment of their supplies and maintain proper evidence when zero-rating is applied. In summary, zero-rating is a beneficial mechanism (especially for exports and essentials), but it comes with strict conditions to prevent abuse, and non-compliance can trigger audits and tax liabilities.

4.3 Exempt Supplies

Exempt supplies are supplies of goods or services on which no VAT is charged at all – not even at 0%. “Exempt” means the supply is outside the scope of VAT output tax: the seller does not add any VAT on the price, and consequently does not account for VAT on that supply to the authorities. However, unlike zero-rated supplies, the supplier of an exempt good or service cannot claim any input VAT on purchases related to that supply. In other words, VAT incurred on inputs used to make exempt supplies becomes a cost to the business, because the VAT system stops at that point (no output tax to offset it against). The concept of exemption is typically applied to certain socially important or sensitive sectors where governments choose not to impose VAT, either to reduce the cost to consumers or because of administrative practicalities or equity considerations.

Important categories of exempt supplies in Zimbabwe include:

Financial services: Most financial services are exempt from VAT. This covers banking and lending services (e.g. interest on loans), insurance services (premiums for life insurance, general insurance), trading of securities or foreign currency, and other intermediary financial transactions. The rationale is that it can be complex to apply VAT to financial margins and also to keep basic financial intermediation costs low for the public. For example, when a bank charges interest or service fees, no VAT is added. Such financial services are listed as exempt supplies under the VAT Act.

Educational services: Services provided by approved educational institutions – such as schools, colleges, and universities – are exempt from VAT. Tuition fees for primary, secondary, and tertiary education are not subject to VAT, to ensure that education is not made more expensive by taxation. For instance, a private school in Zimbabwe does not charge VAT on school fees for students. Educational services are specifically exempt by law, reflecting their social importance.

Medical and healthcare services: The provision of medical services by licensed practitioners (doctors, clinics, hospitals, dentists, etc.) is exempt. When you visit a doctor or buy health services, there is no VAT on the consultation fee or medical treatment cost. This exemption helps keep healthcare more affordable and avoids taxing an essential service. Medicines and drugs may also be zero-rated or exempt under related provisions (often basic drugs are zero-rated as essential goods, whereas the service of the doctor is exempt). Healthcare and medical services are enumerated as exempt supplies.

Residential accommodation: The rental of residential property (dwellings used for private residence) is exempt from VAT. If a landlord leases out a house or apartment to a tenant for use as a home, the rent is not subject to VAT. This exemption ensures that housing costs for individuals are not inflated by VAT. (By contrast, commercial property rent is generally taxable – only residential rentals get the exemption). For example, a homeowner renting out their cottage will not charge VAT on the rent, and similarly developers selling new houses may have certain VAT exemptions on the sale of a dwelling used as a principal residence, according to the law.

Public transport services: The transportation of fare-paying passengers by road, rail, or other means within Zimbabwe is typically exempt. This includes bus fares, commuter omnibus (public taxi) fees, and train tickets for ordinary passengers. The idea is to avoid taxing essential mobility. So, a bus company does not add VAT to the ticket price for a domestic passenger journey. (However, note that international transport of goods or passengers can often be zero-rated as an export service – e.g. cross-border transport may fall under zero-rating by treaty or law. The local commuter transport is exempt as a domestic service.)

Certain utilities and necessities: In addition to the above, Zimbabwe’s VAT regulations exempt some basic utilities and commodities. For example, water supplied for domestic use and electricity for domestic use are exempt from VAT, so households do not pay VAT on their municipal water bills or ZESA electricity bills up to certain usage levels. Basic fuels (like petrol, diesel, paraffin) are also exempt from VAT (they may be subject to other taxes like excise, but not VAT). Furthermore, specific agricultural products and equipment have been exempted via schedules – for instance, certain livestock (cattle, pigs, goats, sheep) and poultry products, as well as specific farming equipment, were recently added to the exemption list to support agriculture. Charitable or donation-based activities are considered as well: donated goods or services given to an association not for gain (charitable organizations) are exempt from VAT, recognizing their non-commercial nature.

Because exempt supplies carry no output tax, a business that deals exclusively in exempt supplies does not register for VAT (registration is only required for taxable supplies above the threshold). In fact, if a trader’s activities are wholly exempt, they are not allowed to register for VAT since there is no tax to collect. The downside for such a trader is they also cannot reclaim any VAT on their expenses. For example, a school that is exempt cannot claim input VAT on its textbooks or laboratory equipment purchases – that VAT becomes part of the school’s costs. This is the impact of exemption on input tax recovery: the “input” VAT sticks and cannot be recovered, unlike in standard-rated or zero-rated scenarios where input VAT can be refunded or credited. Consequently, businesses in exempt sectors often have higher effective costs and may incorporate those into their prices or fees.

It’s worth noting that if a business makes both exempt and taxable supplies (for instance, a hospital might have exempt medical services but also operate a pharmacy selling zero-rated medicines, or a bank might sell taxable secondary services like consulting), then it must apportion input tax. Only the portion of VAT on inputs that relates to taxable (standard or zero-rated) supplies is claimable, and the portion attributable to exempt activities is not claimable. The VAT Act and regulations provide formulas (typically based on turnover) for such apportionment. This ensures that input tax recovery is fair and corresponds only to taxable outputs.

In summary, exempt supplies remove VAT entirely from certain transactions, providing relief to consumers in sectors like finance, education, health, housing, and public transport. The trade-off is that providers in these sectors bear the VAT on their input costs, since they cannot claim refunds. Policymakers use exemptions selectively to support social policy objectives or practical considerations, whereas zero-rating is used for goods/services they want to encourage (like exports and staples) by freeing them from VAT but still keeping suppliers in the VAT system. Understanding the distinction between zero-rated and exempt is crucial for VAT compliance: zero-rated supplies are part of the VAT system (taxed at 0% with credits intact), while exempt supplies are outside the VAT net altogether, with no tax charged and no input credit on related costs. Businesses must correctly classify their supplies into standard-rated, zero-rated, or exempt according to the VAT Act to charge the right rate and to know whether they can reclaim input VAT – misclassification can lead to audits and penalties, as discussed earlier. By mastering these VAT categories and rates, one ensures proper compliance and optimal tax planning in the Zimbabwean context.
